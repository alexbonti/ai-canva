SDLC Step-by-Step Workbook — Build Your Box from Problem to Tested

Study the problem → Design the solution → Implement the box → Test it: the four phases of "One Student, One Box", in the order a project is really built. This is also your workbook — every ✍️ Your work section is yours to fill in: research, diagrams, prompts, test results, report drafts, reflection.
Phases: Four
Budget: 30 hours
Weighting: Research 70% · Build 30%
Assumes: npm run dev works
Workbook: ✍️ fill in as you go

Start here. This document tells you what to do and in what order — and it is where you write your work. You do not need to invent a process: follow the four phases, do the tasks, fill in the ✍️ sections.

The one rule that matters: finish a phase before you start the next. Students who skip Phase 1 (the research) and start coding lose the 70% half of the mark. Coding is only 30%.

5. Phase 4 — Test it: technical tests + user testing (8 h)

Why this phase exists: you know how your box is supposed to work — your users don't. Testing is where you find out whether the box is actually usable, and it gives you the evidence for report §5 and §6.

There are two kinds of testing, and you must do both:

  1. Technical testing (P4-01, P4-02) — does it survive bad input? Six scenarios in §6.
  2. User testing (P4-03 → P4-06) — can three real people use it without you explaining it?

Then watch and write. Every time you explain your box, you hide a bug from yourself.

Be fair to your users: ask permission first, record results as U1 / U2 / U3 (no real names), keep no personal data in your report, and delete any recordings when the project is marked.

7. The three diagrams (and how to draw them simply)

You need three diagrams in total. Draw them on your AI Canva board (it is a whiteboard — boxes and arrows are a diagram), or on paper and photograph them. One page each, maximum.

Rules for every diagram: a title · boxes are nouns · arrows are verbs, labelled · a legend if you use colour · readable at a glance.

1. Problem diagram (P1-06) — what happens today, without your box:

   TODAY (no box)                          WITH YOUR BOX
   ─────────────────                       ─────────────────
   idea in someone's head                  idea ──▶ [ YOUR BOX ] ──▶ a decision
        │                                                              they can act on
        ▼
   pasted into a general chatbot
        │
        ▼
   generic answer → they still don't know what to do

2. Box flow diagram (P2-04a) — where your box sits in a pipeline:

   ┌──────────┐        ┌──────────────┐        ┌──────────────┐
   │ 💡 Idea  │ ─────▶ │  YOUR BOX    │ ─────▶ │ 🔍 Summarize │
   │ (input)  │        │ (does what?) │        │ (next step)  │
   └──────────┘        └──────────────┘        └──────────────┘
        user's            THE THING              proof it
        problem           YOU BUILD              composes

3. Decision / data flow (P2-04b) — what happens inside the box:

   inputs ──▶ prompt template ──▶ model ──▶ output contract ──▶ guardrail ──▶ shown to user
   (which?)    (what it says)     (which?)   (exact format)      (what if    (where in
                                                                 it's wrong?) the box?)

Your research question, in one sentence (P1-07) — fill in the blanks:

For [user] who [situation], does [your box's approach] produce [outcome], better than [what they do today]?

Example: "For a student with a rough project idea who needs to know if it is worth pursuing, does an LLM scoring it on desirability/feasibility/viability produce a judgement they trust, better than asking a general chatbot for an opinion?"

11. Five rules that keep this simple

  1. Finish a phase before starting the next. Design before you build; understand the problem before you design. Skipping Phase 1 is how the 70% gets lost.
  2. One box, no extras. A working, well-researched simple box beats a broken complex one.
  3. Write while you work. Every phase produces report material — Phase 1 §1–2, Phase 2 §3–4, Phase 4 §5–6.
  4. Test with people, not just by yourself. You know how the box is supposed to work; your users don't, and that gap is the most valuable finding in the whole project.
  5. Keep everything. Diagrams, screenshots, test logs, user quotes — they are your evidence, and evidence is what turns an opinion into research.
The finish line. Your box runs in the class mega-pipeline, your three diagrams are in the report, your test log and user table show it was tested on real people, and your research question has an evidence-based answer. That is the whole project — and it is exactly what the rubric rewards.
